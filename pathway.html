1 · Choose your route

Four ways forward, and which one fits you

Most states require a dental degree from a CODA-accredited U.S. or Canadian school before they grant a full license. That's why most international dentists go back to dental school through an advanced standing program. A few states offer alternatives.

Most common · works in every state

A. Advanced standing DDS/DMD

Also called International Dentist Programs (IDP/ITDP/IDDS/PASS). You join a U.S. dental school with credit for your prior training and graduate with a U.S. DDS or DMD, usually in 2 to 3 years. Graduates are treated like any U.S. graduate and can seek licensure in any state.

  • About 47 schools · classes often 20–40 students
  • Highly competitive: ECE, INBDE, TOEFL, CAAPID, then bench test and interview
  • Highest cost, but the most flexibility
State-specific

B. Alternative licensure in certain states

Some states license foreign-trained dentists without a U.S. DDS if they meet other conditions:

  • Minnesota: a "Limited General License" (credential review, INBDE, English test, jurisprudence and clinical exam), then 3 years of supervised practice before a full license
  • Texas: 2 years in a CODA-accredited specialty program (GPR/AEGD don't count)
  • Florida: 2 consecutive years in a CODA "supplemental general dentistry" program
  • Washington: 2 or more academic years in a CODA program with clinical training
  • Massachusetts: a limited "dental intern" license, for example at community health centers

A license from these routes may not transfer to other states. Always confirm with the state board.

Specialists

C. Specialty & residency programs

International dentists can apply to many specialty, GPR and AEGD programs through ADEA PASS or directly. These programs often require the ADAT (200 items, 4.5 hours). In most states a specialty certificate alone does not lead to a general license. Texas is the main exception.

While you prepare

D. Bridge roles

Many applicants build U.S. experience and income first:

  • Dental hygiene licensure for foreign-trained dentists is now allowed in some states, including Indiana and Virginia (from July 2026)
  • Dental assisting (state rules vary), research or lab roles, an MPH/MS
  • Volunteering or observing in a U.S. office, which also helps your letters

⚠️ You'll still see blogs claiming some states license international dentists "with no extra training." That's outdated. California, for example, requires a 2-year program at a board-approved school ending in a DDS/DMD.

2 · The core steps (Route A)

Your step-by-step checklist

  1. 1

    Get a DENTPIN & ECE evaluation

    Order an ECE Course-by-Course report ($199). It works for the INBDE and for most schools. The name must exactly match your DENTPIN.

  2. 2

    Pass the INBDE

    Most programs require a passing result before you apply. Plan for 4–6 months of study.

  3. 3

    TOEFL iBT

    Published minimums range from about 80 to 100 on the old scale, or 4.0 to 5.0 on the new 1–6 band scale. Competitive applicants aim for 100 or more. Many schools don't accept IELTS.

  4. 4

    Build your profile

    U.S. shadowing or volunteering, strong letters (often from a dean, a faculty member and a U.S. dentist), a CV and a focused personal statement.

  5. 5

    Apply via ADEA CAAPID

    The cycle opens in early March. Submit by April or May, since verification takes weeks, and complete each school's supplemental application.

  6. 6

    Bench test & interview

    The step that most often decides offers. Start practising before invitations arrive. This is our specialty.

3 · Evaluations & exams

ECE, INBDE and TOEFL: the details

📄 Credential evaluation (ECE)

  • The JCNDE accepts only ECE for INBDE eligibility. Every non-CODA candidate's degree must be verified.
  • General Report $110 (enough for the exam only) or Course-by-Course $199 (what most schools need, and it covers the INBDE too).
  • CAAPID accepts ECE or WES, but many schools take ECE only. Penn, BU and Columbia are examples.
  • Request transcripts early. Getting documents from your school can take months.
Source: ECE ↗

📝 INBDE

  • Replaced NBDE Parts I & II (Part II ended July 31, 2022).
  • 500 items over 2 days (Day 1 about 8¼ h, Day 2 about 4¼ h), taken within 7 days at the same Prometric center.
  • Pass/fail (a scale score of 75 passes). Failing candidates receive diagnostic feedback.
  • Fee: $890 + $435 international processing fee.
  • Retakes: wait at least 60 days, at most 4 attempts in 12 months.
  • Order: DENTPIN, then ECE verification, then JCNDE eligibility, then schedule the exam.
Source: JCNDE Candidate Guide ↗

🗣️ TOEFL iBT

  • Required by almost every program, and waivers are rare.
  • New scoring since January 21, 2026: a 1–6 band scale, with a comparable 0–120 score shown for about 2 years.
  • Minimums vary: UF 80, UMKC 90, CU Anschutz 94, UCLA 95, NYU/UW/Columbia/Howard 100 (old scale). On the new scale, about 4.0–5.0.
  • IELTS is often not accepted (NYU, Penn, UB). Scores are usually valid for 2 years.
Source: ETS ↗

4 · The application

ADEA CAAPID, demystified

CAAPID is the centralized application most advanced standing programs use. You submit one application, then complete each school's own supplemental application and fee.

  • Opens: early March (March 5, 2026 for the 2026–27 cycle)
  • Deadlines vary widely: from about June 1 (Penn, UW, NYU, UMKC), through July 31 (USC, Howard) and October 1 (UF), to October 15 (BU). Many schools review on a rolling basis, so apply early.
  • Check eligibility first: some programs only accept U.S. citizens or permanent residents (for example UMKC and UF), and a few require the DAT (UF's 4-year DMD)
  • Fees: $264 for the first program and $115 for each additional one, plus school fees (often $75–$185)
  • Evaluations: up to 3 through CAAPID (some schools ask for more)
  • Verification: submit 6–8 weeks before deadlines
ADEA CAAPID Program Finder ↗

What goes into a strong application

  • Personal statement: why U.S. dentistry, what shaped you, and what you'll contribute. Specific, not generic.
  • Letters: often from a dean, a faculty member and a clinical employer or U.S. dentist, on letterhead
  • Experiences: clinical years, teaching, research, volunteering, U.S. shadowing
  • Scores: a passing INBDE and a TOEFL score that clears each school's minimum
  • School list: matched to your profile, deadlines and budget

5 · Selection

Bench test and interview: where offers are won

Shortlisted applicants are invited to a hands-on test and/or an interview. Formats differ: some schools (for example Penn) have no bench test, while others (UB, UW, UCLA) do. Read every invitation carefully.

🦷 The bench (psychomotor) test

  • A timed exam on a typodont in a simulation lab
  • Commonly includes Class II amalgam and composite preps and restorations, crown preparations (often all-ceramic or PFM), and sometimes wax-ups or a rubber dam
  • Graded on outline form, depth, walls, margins, finish, damage to adjacent teeth and timing
  • Examples: USC tests fixed prosthodontics and operative dentistry. UMKC runs a multi-day visit with instrument check-out, lectures, practice time, a bench exam and an interview.
  • Some schools charge a selection or workshop fee (UMKC $2,500, UF Hialeah $1,200)
  • Procedure lists aren't published consistently, so we train for the exact list in your invitation

💬 The interview

  • Faculty interviews, in person or virtual, sometimes a panel. Some schools also use Casper.
  • Tests communication, professionalism, ethics, motivation and fit
  • Expect "Why the U.S.?", "Why our school?", ethical scenarios, patient communication and teamwork questions
  • Generic, memorized or overly long answers are the most common reason strong clinicians lose offers

7 · After graduation

Getting your license

  • Clinical licensure exam: the ADEX dental exam (manikin-based perio, endo, prosthodontic and restorative parts, plus the DLOSCE since June 2026) is accepted in 48 states
  • Some states (for example New York and California) accept a PGY-1 residency in place of a clinical exam
  • State jurisprudence exam, BLS/CPR, a background check and fingerprints
  • Licensure by credentials in many states once you've practised for a set period
ADA licensure by state map ↗

Visa basics (general information only)

  • F-1 student visa for DDS/DMD programs (the school issues the I-20)
  • OPT: 12 months after graduation. Practising still requires a state license.
  • Dentistry is not on the DHS STEM list, so the 24-month STEM OPT extension generally doesn't apply
  • H-1B: subject to the lottery unless the employer is cap-exempt
  • Immigration rules change. Talk to your school's international office or an immigration attorney.

8 · Timeline

A realistic timeline

  1. Months 0–6DENTPIN, ECE evaluation, TOEFL, start INBDE study
  2. Months 6–12Pass the INBDE · gather letters · U.S. experience · start bench practice
  3. March (application year)CAAPID opens. Submit by April or May, plus each school's supplemental application.
  4. Summer–FallBench tests and interviews (invitations can come with only a few weeks' notice)
  5. Following yearStart your 2–3 year program
  6. Final yearADEX and jurisprudence exams, then your license

From first step to license usually takes 3.5 to 5+ years, and longer if you need more than one application cycle.

9 · Avoid these

The most common (and costly) mistakes

❌ Getting a WES evaluation when your schools and the INBDE need ECE, or using a name that doesn't match your DENTPIN
❌ Applying before passing the INBDE at schools that require a passed result
❌ Assuming IELTS is accepted, ignoring the new TOEFL scale, or letting scores expire
❌ Submitting CAAPID late or missing deadlines that range from June to October
❌ Weak or wrong letters: the wrong evaluator type, or not on letterhead
❌ Waiting for an invitation before starting bench test and interview practice
❌ Trusting outdated "no extra training" blogs, or assuming a GPR or specialty certificate gives you a license everywhere
❌ Underestimating cost and visa limits: total cost can approach $400k, and dentistry doesn't qualify for STEM OPT
